\chapter{Background and literature review}
\label{ch:background}

In this chapter, I cover the theoretical background and the prior work that my thesis
builds on. I describe what \ac{EEG} measures and what can disturb it, the main \ac{BCI}
paradigms and why I chose \ac{SSVEP} for this work, existing \ac{SSVEP} spellers, and how
language models are used in them. Finally, I identify the research gap that my thesis
addresses.

\section{EEG basics}
\label{sec:eeg}

\ac{EEG} is a non-invasive method of measuring brain activity~\citep{nunez2006electric}.
Electrodes placed on the scalp record the difference in potential between each electrode
and a reference electrode. The recorded potentials are very small, of the order of
microvolts, so they must be amplified. The \ac{EEG} signal is a mixture of the activity of
many brain regions, and it is easily contaminated by artifacts such as eye blinks, muscle
activity~\citep{goncharova2003emg} and power-line noise~\citep{wang2017benchmark}.
In addition, the frequencies of my \ac{SSVEP} speller (8--15\,Hz) overlap with the alpha
rhythm (8--13\,Hz), which is typically present when the eyes are closed and the subject is
relaxed~\citep{nunez2006electric}.

\section{BCI paradigms}
\label{sec:paradigms}

Of the many \ac{BCI} paradigms, three are most widely used in spellers: P300,
\ac{SSVEP} and \ac{MI}. The P300 paradigm is based on
the brain's response to a rare or unexpected stimulus~\citep{farwell1988talking}. The
\ac{MI} paradigm relies on the user imagining a movement, which changes the \ac{EEG}
rhythms over the motor cortex~\citep{pfurtscheller2001motor}. The \ac{SSVEP} paradigm is
based on the brain's response to a visual stimulus that flickers at a constant
frequency~\citep{chen2015fbcca}. I chose the \ac{SSVEP} paradigm for this work
because it achieves a high \ac{ITR}~\citep{chen2015highspeed,nakanishi2018trca} and needs
little or no calibration data~\citep{lin2006cca}.

\section{SSVEP spellers}
\label{sec:spellers}

In general, an \ac{SSVEP} speller consists of a grid of flickering targets, each with its
own frequency~\citep{nakanishi2018trca}. When the user looks at a target, the \ac{EEG}
recorded over the visual cortex contains a response at the frequency of that target and
its harmonics, so the system can detect which target the user is looking
at~\citep{chen2015fbcca}.

One of the fastest \ac{SSVEP} spellers was presented by \citet{chen2015highspeed}. It had
40 targets whose frequencies were 0.2\,Hz apart. The authors recorded the \ac{EEG} with a
laboratory amplifier from nine electrodes over the parietal and occipital areas, and their
12 subjects reached an average \ac{ITR} of 4.45\,bits/s (about 267\,bits/min) in cued
spelling.

\citet{nakanishi2018trca} improved on this speller with a new classification method,
\ac{TRCA}, which raised the average \ac{ITR} in cued spelling to about 325\,bits/min
(5.4\,bits/s). For more details on \ac{TRCA} and the other classification methods, see
\cref{ch:processing}.

\section{Language models in BCI}
\label{sec:lm-bci}

The simplest way to improve a speller with a language model is to suggest whole words to
the user. \citet{gembler2019dynamic} used this approach: their speller had eight targets on
two levels, with three word suggestions from a bigram model on the first level, and it
reached about 57\,bits/min. Another example is MindChat~\citep{wang2025mindchat}, a speller
that uses a large language model (GPT-4o) to suggest words and whole sentences. Its
keyboard has 40 targets, five of which show suggested words and two whole sentences.

Another use of language models is to improve the classification of \ac{SSVEP} responses by
fusing the output of the classifier with the prediction of the language model.
\citet{zhang2025improving} fused the output of EEGNet, a convolutional neural network, with
that of a character-level \ac{LSTM} language model. However, they tested it only offline,
on the Benchmark dataset~\citep{wang2017benchmark}, where it raised the accuracy by up to
1.19\,\%.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The fastest \ac{SSVEP} spellers are laboratory systems. Spellers that use language models
use them mainly to suggest words, and to my knowledge only \citet{zhang2025improving} used a
language model to improve the classification of \ac{SSVEP} responses, and only offline. My
goal is to use a language model not only to suggest words but also to improve the
classification of \ac{SSVEP} responses, in a real-time system that works with a
consumer-grade \ac{EEG} device.
