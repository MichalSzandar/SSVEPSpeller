\chapter{Signal processing and classification}
\label{ch:processing}

This chapter describes the datasets and the methods I used to process the \ac{EEG} signals
and classify them into the six targets: the Benchmark dataset~\citep{wang2017benchmark} and
my own recordings, the preprocessing pipeline (notch filter, band-pass filter, filter bank),
the classifiers, their implementation, and the evaluation method (cross-validation, accuracy
and \ac{ITR} against window length, statistical tests). The classifiers are \ac{CCA},
\ac{FBCCA} and their normalised variants, \ac{TRCA}, \ac{eTRCA} and \ac{FBeTRCA}, and a
baseline that classifies the \ac{PSD} with \ac{LDA}.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

The Benchmark dataset~\citep{wang2017benchmark} consists of 35 subjects, each with six
blocks of 40 trials, one trial per target in random order. Each trial lasts 6\,s: a 0.5\,s
cue, during which the subject shifts their gaze to the target, 5\,s of flicker and 0.5\,s of
blank screen. The \ac{EEG} was recorded with 64 wet electrodes at 1000\,Hz, and the data were
downsampled to 250\,Hz. The 40 targets are arranged in a 5-by-8 grid and flicker at
frequencies from 8 to 15.8\,Hz in steps of 0.2\,Hz, with phases from 0 to $1.5\pi$ in steps
of $0.5\pi$~\citep{chen2015highspeed}. The dataset is publicly available at
\url{https://bci.med.tsinghua.edu.cn/}. I used the nine parieto-occipital channels Pz, PO5,
PO3, POz, PO4, PO6, O1, Oz and O2, as \citet{nakanishi2018trca} did, and only the six targets
at the speller frequencies (\cref{tab:targets}), which lie on the Benchmark grid: 36 trials
per subject, six per target. The analysis window starts 0.14\,s after the flicker onset, the
visual latency used by \citet{chen2015highspeed} and \citet{nakanishi2018trca}.

To verify my implementations of \ac{CCA}, \ac{FBCCA} and the \ac{TRCA} family, I tested them
on all 40 targets of the Benchmark dataset and obtained roughly the same accuracies as those
reported by \citet{nakanishi2018trca} (\cref{sec:benchmark}).

I also recorded my own data with the cap described in \cref{sec:hardware}, in two sessions
on different days with myself as the subject. Each session followed the calibration protocol
of \cref{sec:calibration}: ten blocks of six trials, one trial per target in random order.
Each trial consists of a 2\,s cue and 5\,s of flicker. It is cut into a 6\,s epoch with the
same layout as a Benchmark epoch: 0.5\,s before the flicker onset, the 5\,s of flicker and
0.5\,s after it, so the same processing runs on both datasets. The analysis window starts
0.7\,s after the onset marker instead of 0.14\,s, because in the first session the timestamps
of the cap ran about 0.5\,s ahead of the markers. A latency of 0.7\,s gave the best accuracy
in that session and close to the best in the second (\cref{sec:own-recordings}).

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The raw \ac{EEG} signal is noisy. Dry electrodes have a higher impedance than gel electrodes,
the system uses only eight of the 16 channels of the cap, and the cap has no PO electrodes
(\cref{sec:hardware}), so the signal quality is lower than
in laboratory recordings~\citep{mathewson2017dry}. In addition, the recorded signal contains
activity that is not related to the stimulus: eye blinks and eye movements, muscle activity
of the face and neck, and the ongoing rhythms of the brain. Filtering removes only the part
of it that lies outside the band of the \ac{SSVEP}: power-line interference at 50\,Hz, slow
drifts of the electrode potentials, most of the energy of blinks and eye movements, which is
concentrated at the lowest frequencies, and noise above the upper edge of the band-pass.
\missing{citation for the low-frequency spectrum of blinks and eye movements}
Muscle activity has a broad spectrum, from a few hertz to over 200\,Hz, and is strongest
between about 20 and 80\,Hz~\citep{goncharova2003emg}, so most of it stays in the filtered
signal. The alpha rhythm (8--13\,Hz) stays as well, because it overlaps the four lowest
target frequencies (8.0, 9.4, 10.8 and 12.2\,Hz). What filtering leaves is reduced later by
the spatial filters of \ac{CCA} and \ac{TRCA}, which weight the channels so that the
\ac{SSVEP} stands out, and by leaving the noisiest trials and channels out of training.

Each epoch passes through three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A zero-phase notch filter at 50\,Hz (quality factor 30) removes the power-line
          interference. It is applied only to my own recordings, because the Benchmark data
          were notch-filtered when they were recorded~\citep{wang2017benchmark}.
    \item The methods without a filter bank (\ac{CCA}, \ac{TRCA}, \ac{eTRCA}, the baseline
          and the normalised \ac{CCA}) use a single band-pass filter between 7 and 90\,Hz, a
          fourth-order Butterworth filter applied forwards and backwards, so with zero phase.
          The filter-bank methods (\ac{FBCCA}, its normalised variant and \ac{FBeTRCA}) use
          the filter bank instead: $N_b = 5$ zero-phase Chebyshev type~I band-pass filters,
          where sub-band~$m$ passes from $(8m - 2)$\,Hz to 90\,Hz, that is from 6, 14, 22, 30
          and 38\,Hz. The lower edges rise in steps of 8\,Hz, the lowest target frequency, so
          each higher sub-band leaves out more of the low-frequency content and relies more on
          the harmonics. The filter design follows the public code of
          \citet{nakanishi2018trca}.
    \item The analysis window of length~$T$ is cut out of the filtered epoch, starting at the
          stimulus onset plus the visual latency. Filtering the longer epoch before cutting
          keeps the edge effects of the filters outside the window.
\end{enumerate}

\section{CCA and FBCCA}
\label{sec:cca}

\ac{CCA}~\citep{lin2006cca} and \ac{FBCCA}~\citep{chen2015fbcca} are widely used for
\ac{SSVEP} classification. Both measure how strongly the processed \ac{EEG} signal correlates
with reference signals, which are sine and cosine waves at the target frequency and its
harmonics. For target~$k$, the reference signals form the matrix
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Y}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \sin(2\pi f_k \mathbf{t}) \\
        \cos(2\pi f_k \mathbf{t}) \\
        \sin(2\pi \cdot 2 f_k \mathbf{t}) \\
        \cos(2\pi \cdot 2 f_k \mathbf{t}) \\
        \vdots \\
        \sin(2\pi N_h f_k \mathbf{t}) \\
        \cos(2\pi N_h f_k \mathbf{t})
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_h \times N_s},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{t} = \left[0, \frac{1}{f_s}, \frac{2}{f_s}, \dots, \frac{N_s - 1}{f_s}\right],
    \label{eq:cca-references}
\end{equation}
where $f_k$ is the frequency of target~$k$, $\mathbf{t}$ is the row vector of sample times,
$f_s$ is the sampling rate, $N_s = T f_s$ is the number of samples in a window of length~$T$,
and $N_h$ is the number of harmonics ($N_h = 5$ in this work). The sine and cosine are applied
element-wise, so each row of $\mathbf{Y}_k$ is one reference signal of length $N_s$.
One such matrix is generated for each of the $N_t$ targets.

For each target, \ac{CCA} calculates the largest canonical correlation coefficient $\rho_k$
between $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}_k$, where $\mathbf{X}$ is the processed \ac{EEG} window
(one row per channel) and $\mathbf{Y}_k$ is the reference matrix of target~$k$, whose
frequency is given in \cref{tab:targets}. The target with the highest correlation is selected.

\ac{FBCCA} applies \ac{CCA} to each sub-band of the filter bank (\cref{sec:preprocessing})
separately and combines the results with a weighted sum. The weight of sub-band~$m$ is
\begin{equation}
    w_m = m^{-a} + b,
    \label{eq:fb-weights}
\end{equation}
where $a$ and $b$ are two constants shared by all sub-bands. I used $a = 1.25$ and
$b = 0.25$, the values that \citet{chen2015fbcca} chose by optimising the accuracy on their
offline data. The weights are therefore fixed in advance and do not depend on the recorded
data. The score of each target is the weighted sum of its squared correlations over the
sub-bands,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \rho_{1,1}^2 & \rho_{1,2}^2 & \dots & \rho_{1,N_b}^2 \\
        \rho_{2,1}^2 & \rho_{2,2}^2 & \dots & \rho_{2,N_b}^2 \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \rho_{N_t,1}^2 & \rho_{N_t,2}^2 & \dots & \rho_{N_t,N_b}^2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        w_1 \\
        w_2 \\
        \vdots \\
        w_{N_b}
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        S_1 \\
        S_2 \\
        \vdots \\
        S_{N_t}
    \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:fbcca-score}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{k,m}$ is the canonical correlation between the \ac{EEG} in sub-band~$m$ and the
reference signals of target~$k$, and $S_k$ is the score of target~$k$. The target with the
highest score is selected.

I also implemented normalised variants of \ac{CCA} and \ac{FBCCA}, which normalise the score
of each target by the sum of the scores of all targets. This helps to reduce the effect of

\section{The TRCA family}
\label{sec:trca}

The TRCA family of methods was originally proposed by \citet{tanaka2013trca} for \ac{NIRS} 
and later applied to \ac{SSVEP} by \citet{nakanishi2018trca}. Unlike \ac{CCA}, which compares 
the \ac{EEG} with artificial sine waves and does not require training, \ac{TRCA} learns 
from a set of calibration trials gathered from the user before the online experiment.
The key idea behind \ac{TRCA} is that the \ac{SSVEP} response is time-locked to the stimulus, 
so that means that every trial of a given target should have the same waveform and phase, 
wheras other sources of noise such as alpha rhythms, muscle activity, and eye blinks differ 
from trial to trial. Therefore, \ac{TRCA} seeks to find a spatial filter that maximizes the 
similarity of the trials for each target, effectively enhancing the \ac{SSVEP} signal while 
suppressing noise.

The spatial filter of \ac{TRCA} is computed for each target separately. 
Let $\mathbf{X}_k^{(h)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c \times N_s}$ be the $h$-th training trial of 
target~$k$, with the mean of each channel removed, where $N_c$ is the number of channels 
and $N_s$ is the number of samples in the analysis window. 
The filter $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c}$ has one weight per channel, and the filtered 
trial is $\mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{X}_k^{(h)}$, a single time series. The matrices

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{C}_k = \sum_{h_1 \neq h_2} \mathbf{X}_k^{(h_1)} \mathbf{X}_k^{(h_2)\top},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{Q}_k = \sum_{h=1}^{N_\mathrm{tr}} \mathbf{X}_k^{(h)} \mathbf{X}_k^{(h)\top},
    \label{eq:trca-matrices}
\end{equation}

represent the agreement of the filtered trials and their total variance, respectively.

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w}_k = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\mathbf{w}}
    \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{C}_k \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{Q}_k \mathbf{w}}.
    \label{eq:trca-objective}
\end{equation}

The solution of \cref{eq:trca-objective} is the eigenvector of $\mathbf{Q}_k^{-1} \mathbf{C}_k$ 
with the largest eigenvalue~\citep{nakanishi2018trca}. The filter $\mathbf{w}_k$ is computed 
for each target~$k$, resulting in $N_t$ filters in total.

The TRCA template of target~$k$ is the mean of its training trials, because averaging cancels 
the noise and leaves the time-locked \ac{SSVEP} response. 
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathbf{X}}_k = \frac{1}{N_\mathrm{tr}} \sum_{h=1}^{N_\mathrm{tr}} \mathbf{X}_k^{(h)},
    \qquad
    \rho_k = \operatorname{corr}\left(\mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{X},\,
                                      \mathbf{w}_k^\top \bar{\mathbf{X}}_k\right),
    \label{eq:trca-score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X}$ is the test window, and $\rho_k$ is the Pearson correlation coefficient between 
the filtered test window and the filtered template of target~$k$. The target with the highest 
correlation is selected. It works on short windows because the template is the user's own averaged response, with its real waveform,
harmonics, phase and latency, not a pure sine wave.

The problem with \ac{TRCA} is that each filter is learnt from only a few training trials, so it contains noise.
\ac{eTRCA} adresses this issue by using the fact that the \ac{SSVEP} responses of all targets come from the same 
area of the visual cortex, so their filters should be similar. It learns all $N_t$ filters at once, 
by maximising the sum of the correlations of all pairs of filtered trials of all targets~\citep{nakanishi2018trca}. 
The resulting filters are less noisy and give a higher accuracy than \ac{TRCA}.
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W} = \left[\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{N_t}\right]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c \times N_t},
    \qquad
    \rho_k = \operatorname{corr}\left(\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{X}),\,
                                      \operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{W}^\top \bar{\mathbf{X}}_k)\right),
    \label{eq:etrca-score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}$ is the matrix of all $N_t$ filters, and $\operatorname{vec}$ stacks the rows of a matrix into one vector.

FB-eTRCA is yet another extension of \ac{TRCA} that combines \ac{eTRCA} with the filter bank of \ac{FBCCA}.
It runs \ac{eTRCA} in each of the $N_b$ sub-bands of the filter bank, with its own filters and templates per sub-band, and combines the scores
with the weights of \cref{eq:fb-weights}:
\begin{equation}
    S_k = \sum_{m=1}^{N_b} w_m \, \rho_{k,m},
    \label{eq:fbetrca-score}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{k,m}$ is the \ac{eTRCA} correlation of target~$k$ in sub-band~$m$, and $S_k$ is the score of target~$k$.
Contrary to \ac{FBCCA}, the correlations are not squared, because a negative correlation is evidence against the target, 
and squaring it would turn it into evidence for it. The target with the highest score is selected.

TRCA and its extensions need calibration data from the same session, because training on another session 
lowered the accuracy of \ac{eTRCA} in my pilot recordings. They also need at least two training 
trials per target, because \ac{TRCA} cannot compute a filter with only one trial. Finally, 
they require test windows of the same length and start as the training windows, and precise onset 
markers: trials that are not aligned in time blur the template and reduce the correlation with the test window.

\section{Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}

In order to compare \ac{CCA} and \ac{TRCA} with a simpler approach, I implemented a baseline classifier
that uses the \ac{PSD} of the \ac{EEG} signal as features and classifies them with \ac{LDA}.
The \ac{PSD} is estimated with Welch's method~\citep{welch1967psd}, which here uses a single segment: the whole
window. The estimate is therefore a periodogram of the whole window multiplied by a Hann
window, with no overlapping segments and no averaging. The window is zero-padded to a length
of 10\,s (2500 samples), so the frequencies of the periodogram lie on a 0.1\,Hz grid. Without
padding they would be $f_s / N_s$ apart, for example 1\,Hz for a 1\,s window, and would miss
most target frequencies. With padding, every target frequency and its harmonics, which are all
multiples of 0.1\,Hz, fall on the grid.
I chose to use the Hann-window periodogram instead of the multitaper method~\citep{thomson1982multitaper}, because it is simpler and faster to compute,
and it provides sufficient frequency resolution for the target frequencies and their harmonics.

The logarithms of the \ac{PSD} at the target frequencies and their harmonics are the input
features of the \ac{LDA} classifier. I use the logarithm because the logarithm of the power is
closer to a normal distribution than the power itself~\citep{gasser1982normal}, and \ac{LDA}
assumes normally distributed features.

The baseline uses only the fundamental and the second harmonic of each target. Each trial
therefore gives a feature vector of length $2 N_t N_c$: the log power at each of the $N_t$
target frequencies and at its second harmonic, in each of the $N_c$ channels. That is 96
features for my recordings and 108 for the Benchmark dataset.

\ac{LDA} assumes that the features of every target are normally distributed with the same
covariance matrix, and it separates the targets with linear decision boundaries computed from
the means of the targets and this covariance matrix. Each fold of the cross-validation
(\cref{sec:evaluation}) has at most 54 training trials for my recordings and 30 for Benchmark,
fewer than the number of features. With fewer trials than features, the sample covariance
matrix $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ is singular and cannot be inverted. I therefore use shrinkage
\ac{LDA}~\citep{blankertz2011erp}, which replaces $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ with
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\bm{\Sigma}} = (1 - \gamma)\,\hat{\bm{\Sigma}} + \gamma \nu \mathbf{I},
    \qquad
    \nu = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})}{d},
    \label{eq:shrinkage}
\end{equation}
where $d = 2 N_t N_c$ is the number of features, $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix and $\nu$
is the average variance of the features. The shrunk matrix $\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}$ can always be
inverted. The shrinkage parameter $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is computed from the training trials with
the analytical formula of \citet{ledoit2004shrinkage}, so it needs no extra cross-validation.
Shrinking towards $\nu \mathbf{I}$ treats all features alike only if they have the same scale.
Before \ac{LDA}, every feature is therefore standardised: its mean is subtracted and it is
divided by its standard deviation, both computed on the training trials only. Both steps use
the implementations in scikit-learn~\citep{pedregosa2011sklearn}.

I use this method as a baseline because, unlike \ac{CCA} and \ac{TRCA}, it has no spatial
filter: every channel gives its own features. Unlike \ac{TRCA}, it also ignores the phase and
the waveform of the response and uses only the power at the target frequencies.

\section{Evaluation method}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The basic measure of a classifier is its accuracy: the proportion of trials in which it selects
the correct target. A classifier that guesses at random selects the correct one of the six
targets in 1/6 of the trials, so the chance level is about 16.7\,\%.

The main measure is the \ac{ITR}~\citep{wolpaw2002bci}: the amount of information, in bits,
that the user can transmit per minute. It combines the accuracy and the speed of the system in
one number, and it is the usual measure for comparing \ac{SSVEP}
spellers~\citep{chen2015highspeed,nakanishi2018trca}. The number of bits per selection is
\begin{equation}
    B = \log_2 N_t + P \log_2 P + (1 - P) \log_2\left(\frac{1 - P}{N_t - 1}\right),
    \label{eq:bits}
\end{equation}
where $N_t$ is the number of targets and $P$ is the accuracy, and the \ac{ITR} in bits per
minute is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ITR} = \frac{60\,B}{T_\mathrm{sel}},
    \label{eq:itr}
\end{equation}
where $T_\mathrm{sel}$ is the time per selection in seconds. For $P = 1$, the number of bits is
$B = \log_2 N_t$, and at or below the chance level the \ac{ITR} is set to zero. In the offline
evaluation, $T_\mathrm{sel} = T + 0.5$\,s: the window length plus the 0.5\,s gaze shift of the
speller (\cref{sec:online}). In the online experiment, $T_\mathrm{sel}$ is the measured time
from one selection to the next (\cref{sec:online-results}).

To make sure that classifiers were not tested on the same trials they were trained on, I 
used leave-one-block-out cross-validation: one block of trials is left out for testing, 
and the classifier is trained on the remaining blocks. This is repeated for every block, 
and the accuracy is mean over all folds. In this context a block is a set of 6 trials, one per target, 
and a fold is one iteration of the cross-validation.

Before a classifier is fitted, its training trials pass through artifact rejection. During
some trials an electrode may lose contact with the scalp, or the user may blink or move, and
the signal then contains large swings that are not related to the stimulus. A few such trials
can distort what a classifier learns from the training data, for example the templates of
\ac{TRCA} or the covariance matrix of \ac{LDA}, so the worst channels and trials are left out
of training. The checks work on the filtered and cropped window (for the filter-bank methods,
on the first sub-band). Both are relative: each signal is compared with the median of the
same training data, not with a fixed limit in microvolts, so the same thresholds work for the
dry electrodes of the cap and for the wet electrodes of the Benchmark dataset. The median is
used because a few artifact trials hardly change it.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Channel check.} The level of a channel is the median, over the training
          trials, of its standard deviation within a trial. A channel whose level is more than
          three times, or less than one third of, the median level of all channels is dropped.
          This removes an electrode that is noisy or flat for the whole session, and the
          classifier then uses only the remaining channels, both in training and in testing.
    \item \textbf{Trial check.} On the remaining channels, the peak-to-peak amplitude of each
          channel in a trial is divided by the median peak-to-peak amplitude of that channel
          over the training trials. The ratio of a trial is the largest ratio of its channels,
          so a single broken electrode is enough to flag the trial. A trial whose ratio is
          above five is left out of training. At least two trials of each target are always
          kept, the ones with the lowest ratios, because \ac{TRCA} needs at least two trials
          per target.
\end{itemize}
I set both thresholds high, so that only gross artifacts such as a lost contact are removed,
not the normal variation between trials (\cref{sec:benchmark,sec:own-recordings}). Only the
training trials are checked: every test trial is classified, because in the online
experiment a selection cannot be skipped either. The same step is applied when the online
classifier is fitted on the calibration data.

To determine the best window length for each method, I evaluated the accuracy and \ac{ITR} 
for window lengths from 0.5 to 5\,s in steps of 0.5\,s using window-sweeping: the window 
starts at the stimulus onset and ends at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, \dots, 5.0\,s.
